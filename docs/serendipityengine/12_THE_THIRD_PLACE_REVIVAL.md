# The Third Place Revival

The single most consequential physical fact about modern American life — and increasingly Western European, urban East Asian, and Latin American life — is the collapse of **third places**: the informal, free-or-cheap, voluntary, repeated-encounter venues where citizens become known to each other outside the family (1st) and outside work (2nd). Ray Oldenburg's *The Great Good Place* (1989) named the category; Robert Putnam's *Bowling Alone* (2000) quantified its decline; the U.S. Surgeon General's 2023 loneliness advisory made the cost a national emergency.

This document specifies the platform's strategy for **reviving** third places — not by building new ones from scratch, but by making the existing ones *functional* again, and by partnering them into a city-scale network of *koinōnia* infrastructure.

## 1. The thesis

Most cities still have third-place *real estate* — cafes, bars, parks, libraries, community centers, places of worship, recreation leagues, churches' fellowship halls, bookstores, dance halls, bowling alleys, neighborhood restaurants. What they lack is *traffic*, *repetition*, and *programming*. The platform's serendipity engine is precisely a traffic-and-programming engine. By directing 1.5+ in-person events per citizen per week into venue partners, we restore the conditions under which a venue can again be a third place: regular, recognizable, and full of the same returning faces.

Conversely: without real venues, the platform is a calendar app. The two are co-essential — the algorithm needs places to send people; the places need people to send.

## 3. The Venue Partnership Program

### 3.1 Who qualifies as a Partner Venue

A Partner Venue is a physical third place that meets a small standard:

- **Open to the public** without membership fee (a cafe is fine; a private club is not, unless they create a public floor).
- **Walkable from at least 300 citizens** of a polis — i.e., dense enough that gatherings can be ad-hoc.
- **Conducive to conversation** — moderate noise floor, separable from a TV / music PA, with movable chairs or at least a long shared table.
- **Owned independently** (not part of a national chain larger than ~ 25 locations). The platform's relationship is with neighborhood-anchored businesses, not Starbucks.
- **Willing to honor the platform's behavioral norms** — phone basket at the table for Symposium, quiet 90-minute window for the structured part of a Symposium, no upselling attendees, no commercial pitch during a gathering.

The set is large: every American city has hundreds of qualifying venues; every European city has thousands.

### 3.2 The Partnership terms (the business arrangement)

- **The platform sends the venue verified citizens** at specific dates and times in pre-booked group reservations (typically 5-7 for a Symposium, 8-15 for a Gymnasium post-event meal, 10-25 for a Theater).
- **The venue offers a modest group rate** — fixed price per attendee for a meal-or-drinks-package, ~10-20% under retail (modeled on Timeleft's restaurant arrangements). Citizens pay the venue directly at the gathering; the platform never touches food/drink money.
- **The venue commits to honoring the behavioral norms** above.
- **The venue gets a steady, predictable, weekday-evening flow** of paying customers in shoulder-time slots when they would otherwise be empty.
- **The platform takes a small partnership fee** from the venue per attendee per gathering (≤ 8%) — this is one of the two revenue streams (see `13`). The fee is structured to be venue-positive (the venue's marginal revenue from a 6-top Symposium at a quiet Tuesday 7pm is net positive even after the fee).
- **No exclusivity.** Venues partner with the platform, with Timeleft, with Meetup, with neighborhood associations. Healthy ecosystem.

### 3.3 Venue typology

Per Oldenburg, third places vary by character. We classify venue partners into typologies that map cleanly to the Five Formats:

| Venue type | Maps to format(s) |
|---|---|
| Cafe / coffeehouse / tea-room | Symposium, Theater (small), Aleatory |
| Bistro / neighborhood restaurant / taverna | Symposium, Festival mini |
| Pub / wine bar / bar with seated tables (not stand-and-drink) | Symposium (with care; no Newcomers on first event), Agora |
| Public library / bookstore with seating | Theater, Chevruta meetup |
| Community center / church fellowship hall / mosque hall / temple hall | Theater (large), Agora, Festival |
| Park / public square / running trail | Gymnasium, Festival |
| Recreation center / gym / bouldering wall / pool / dance studio | Gymnasium |
| Theatre / music venue / cinema (post-show conversation area) | Theater (post-performance) |
| Farmer's market / open-air market / public plaza | Festival, Aleatory |

A polis cultivates ~ 50-200 Partner Venues across these types — enough that any neighborhood has a 10-minute walk to a venue suitable for the format the citizen prefers.

### 3.4 Venue partner member of the cooperative

Per `14`: venue partners are *members* of the multi-stakeholder cooperative that owns the platform. They have governance seats (proportional but capped). They co-shape the venue program. This prevents the "platform extracts from venue" capture pattern that hollowed out Yelp, OpenTable, and Resy.

## 4. The Civic Venue Program (public partners)

For non-commercial third places — public libraries, parks, community centers, schools after hours, fellowship halls — we negotiate civic-partnership agreements with the operating institution. No fee in either direction; the platform sends programming, the venue provides space, both contribute to the polis. (Modeled on the "public library as third place" advocacy of Eric Klinenberg, *Palaces for the People*.)

## 5. The new-venue catalyst program (where third places are missing)

Some neighborhoods (post-1970 suburbs, exurban developments, certain redeveloped downtowns) have *no* third places within walking distance. The platform's response, in concert with local cooperative chapters:

- **Identification:** mapping every polis's third-place deserts (neighborhoods with > 500 citizens but < 1 walkable third place).
- **Activation:** sponsoring pop-up Symposia at non-traditional venues (a neighbor's living room, a community garden, a parking lot with portable furniture) to demonstrate demand.
- **Catalyst grants:** small no-strings grants ($5k-$25k) from the platform's civic fund (see `13` §5) to neighborhood entrepreneurs opening a cafe, bookstore, bistro, or co-working space in a third-place desert.
- **Cooperative ownership pilots:** in extreme cases, the platform helps form a citizen-owned cooperative cafe / library / community space. (Modeled on the British "Plunkett Foundation" rural pub-cooperative program, which has saved ~ 150 closing village pubs since 2008.)

This is the long-arc work. It produces nothing for several years. It produces compounding civic substrate over decades.

## 6. The Venue Code of Conduct

Posted at every Partner Venue, taught at every Citizenship Reading, summarized verbally by every convener at every gathering:

1. **Phones in the basket** at the table during Symposium (5–7) and Theater (10–25) gatherings. Phones permitted at Agora and Gymnasium but not for use during structured parts.
2. **No selling, soliciting, or pitching** — commercial, political, religious, romantic — during a gathering.
3. **No recording, no photography, no quoting** — applies absolutely to Agora, defaults to "ask permission" for other formats.
4. **No identity disclosure of fellow attendees** off-platform without their consent — last names, employers, etc.
5. **Mutual welcome** of newcomers; one Established citizen makes the first introduction at each Symposium.
6. **Equal speech-time** — no monopolizing; the convener may gently redirect.
7. **The convener is the host** — their judgment on table dynamics is honored.
8. **Disagreements end the night civilly** — no escalation, no continuation off-platform; report to a sortitioned advisor if needed.

Violations: convener may quietly remove an attendee from the gathering; sortitioned-review-board handles patterns; first serious violation = 90-day pause; second = lifetime ban.

## 7. The venue-as-witness role

A Partner Venue's staff are quiet witnesses to the platform's actual effects. They see who returns. They see which Symposia generate laughter and which sit silent. They see who is alone before the gathering and who leaves with a new friend. Their qualitative observation is solicited periodically (a sortitioned advisor visits each Partner Venue quarterly for a 30-minute conversation). The aggregate of venue impressions is one of the most reliable signals of whether the platform is actually working in a city.

## 8. The geographic radius problem

The platform is meaningless if it sends a citizen across town to dinner. The matching algorithm respects a per-citizen geographic radius (default 1.5mi for Symposium, 3mi for Gymnasium, 5mi for Agora, citywide for Festival) and citizens may tune this. Newcomer matches stay tighter.

This implies: cities expand polis-by-polis through *neighborhoods*, not by carpet-bombing the whole metro at once. A polis launches first in 2-3 dense neighborhoods, builds Partner Venue density there, then expands to adjacent neighborhoods. (See `18`.)
